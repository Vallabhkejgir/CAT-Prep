## Concept 1: Traps #1 & #2 — Extreme Quantifiers (`only, always`) & Unauthorized Comparisons (`more than`)

### 1. Concept & Rule in Plain English
When you narrow an RC question down to 2 options, 70% of the time the wrong option commits one of two mechanical sins:
1. **Trap #1 (Extreme Quantifier)**: The passage says *"X **can** contribute to Y"* or *"X **often** causes Y"*, and the option inflates it to *"X is the **sole / primary / overriding** cause of Y"* or *"X **always / inevitably** causes Y"*.
2. **Trap #2 (Unauthorized Comparison)**: The passage mentions both $X$ and $Y$ (e.g., *"small dams are not inconsequential"* and *"large dams displace people"*), and the option invents a ranking (*"small dams are **safer than** large dams"* or *"X is **more effective than** Y"*) that was never made in the text!

### 2. When to Use It (Question / Text Cue)
- Every Inference, Main Idea, and Specific Detail question when scanning options for red-flag words.

### 3. General Mini-Passage Example (Single Concept)
> *"During the 2020 lockdown, the sudden cancellation of live sports drove millions of displaced sports bettors into retail stock trading apps, contributing to the rapid rebound in speculative technology shares alongside central-bank liquidity injections."*
>
> **Question**: Which of the following can be inferred from the statement above?
> - **(A)** Displaced sports bettors were the overriding cause of the 2020 stock market recovery across all asset classes.
> - **(B)** Retail stock trading apps attracted more users during 2020 than central banks anticipated.
> - **(C)** Behavioral shifts among non-traditional participants played a role in the 2020 rebound of speculative tech stocks.

### 4. Step-by-Step Walkthrough (Two Ways)
- **Way 1 — Defect Tagging**:
  - **(A)**: **Trap #1 (Extreme Quantifier)** — *"overriding cause... across all asset classes"* distorts *"contributing to... speculative technology shares alongside central-bank liquidity"*.
  - **(B)**: **Trap #2 (Unauthorized Comparison)** — *"more users... than central banks anticipated"* is never compared in the text.
  - **(C)**: **Correct!** Modest, accurate restatement of *"contributing to the rapid rebound in speculative technology shares"*.

### 5. The 1 Common Mistake to Avoid
- **Trap**: Picking **(A)** because *"displaced sports bettors"* and *"rebound"* are vivid keywords from the sentence, while ignoring the poison words **`overriding`** and **`all`**!

## Concept 2: Traps #3, #5 & #6 — Causal Reversal, Tail-End Poison & Descriptive $\to$ Normative (`should`) Shifts

### 1. Concept & Rule in Plain English
- **Trap #3 (Causal Reversal)**: Text says $A \to B$; option flips it to $B \to A$.
- **Trap #5 (Half-Right, Tail-End Poison)**: The first 12 words of the option are 100% faithful to the passage, OR the option starts with the right philosophical label (*"Following deontological ethics..."*), and then the **last 5 words** sneak in the exact opposite definition (*"...by maximizing total happiness for the greatest number"*)! **Always read the second half of every option carefully!**
- **Trap #6 (Descriptive $\to$ Normative Shift)**: Text describes what *is* happening; option says governments *should / must / ought to* ban or mandate it.

### 2. When to Use It (Question / Text Cue)
- Close 2-option showdowns and philosophical/policy application questions (`CAT 2025 VARC Slot 3 Q04`).

### 3. General Mini-Passage Example (Single Concept)
> *"Utilitarianism judges every policy solely by whether it maximizes total aggregate happiness across society, whereas prioritarianism assigns extra moral weight to improving the wellbeing of the worst-off individuals even if total aggregate gains are smaller."*
>
> **Question**: Which policy decision departs from utilitarianism as defined above?
> - **(A)** Following deontological duty, the board selected the hospital location that delivered the highest total health benefit to the entire city.
> - **(B)** The ministry funded rural clinics for the poorest district rather than a metro hospital that would have generated larger total health gains.

### 4. Step-by-Step Walkthrough (Two Ways)
- **Way 1 — Second-Half Inspection**:
  - Look at **(A)**: Starts with the non-utilitarian label *"Following deontological duty"*, **BUT** read the second half: *"...selected the location that delivered the highest total health benefit to the entire city"*—that is 100% utilitarian maximization! (**Half-Right, Half-Poisoned Trap!**)
  - Look at **(B)**: Prioritizes the poorest district *"rather than... larger total health gains"* $\implies$ genuinely departs from utilitarianism!

### 5. The 1 Common Mistake to Avoid
- **Trap**: Stopping at the label *"deontological duty"* in the first 3 words of **(A)** without reading the tail end of the sentence!

## Concept 3: CAT Inference vs. Real-World Guesswork (Strict Entailment & Contrapositive Rule)

### 1. Concept & Rule in Plain English
In everyday life, "infer" means "make an educated guess." In CAT RC, **"infer" means "prove 100% from the text without adding a single outside assumption"**:
- If the text says *"All $A$ are $B$"* ($A \implies B$), the valid inference is the **Contrapositive**: *"Anything that is NOT $B$ cannot be $A$"* ($\neg B \implies \neg A$).
- If the text says *"Both literary and scientific approaches are necessary, working in unison, and subjective experience is no less real than a map"*, you can infer that *neither approach alone is sufficient*—you **cannot** infer that *subjective experience can replace a map*!

### 2. When to Use It (Question / Text Cue)
- *"Which of the following can be rightly inferred from the passage?"*

### 3. General Mini-Passage Example (Single Concept)
> *"No central bank can maintain a fixed exchange rate while simultaneously allowing free cross-border capital flows and conducting an independent monetary policy."*
>
> **Question**: Which of the following can be inferred from the statement above?
> - **(A)** Fixed exchange rates are more harmful to developing economies than free capital flows.
> - **(B)** If a country allows free cross-border capital flows and maintains a fixed exchange rate, its central bank cannot conduct an independent monetary policy.
> - **(C)** Most central banks prefer independent monetary policy over fixed exchange rates.

### 4. Step-by-Step Walkthrough (Two Ways)
- **Way 1 — Logical Entailment Check**:
  - Text rule: You cannot have all 3 of `{Fixed Rate, Free Capital, Independent Monetary Policy}` at the same time.
  - Therefore, if a country has `{Fixed Rate + Free Capital}`, it **cannot** have `{Independent Monetary Policy}` $\implies$ **Option (B)** is 100% mathematically entailed!
  - **(A)** and **(C)** introduce unstated comparisons/preferences.

### 5. The 1 Common Mistake to Avoid
- **Trap**: Picking an option that sounds like smart economics trivia (`C`) instead of the strict logical consequence (`B`).

## Concept 4: Critical Reasoning in RC — Necessary Assumption (Negation Test), Strengthen & Weaken

### 1. Concept & Rule in Plain English
Every Critical Reasoning question in RC targets the link between **Premise (Evidence)** and **Conclusion (Claim)**:
- **Necessary Assumption (Use the Negation Test!)**: An unstated premise that the author *must* believe for the argument to work. To verify between 2 options, **negate the option** (insert *"NOT"*): if negating the option **destroys** the author's conclusion, it is the correct Necessary Assumption!
- **Strengthen**: Shows the cause produces the effect, or rules out an alternative explanation.
- **Weaken**: Shows an alternative cause, reverse causation, or that the cause occurs without the effect.

### 2. When to Use It (Question / Text Cue)
- *"Which of the following is an unstated assumption required by the author's argument?"* or *"Which of the following, if true, most undermines the researcher's inference?"*

### 3. General Mini-Passage Example (Single Concept)
> *"Because remote workers spend zero time commuting in traffic, companies that switch to permanent remote work will inevitably see a 15% increase in daily employee output."*
>
> **Question**: The argument above relies on which of the following assumptions?
> - **(A)** Commuting in traffic is the most stressful part of an employee's workday.
> - **(B)** Remote workers do not lose more productive time to home distractions or coordination delays than they save by not commuting.

### 4. Step-by-Step Walkthrough (Two Ways)
- **Apply the Negation Test**:
  - Negate **(A)**: *"Commuting is NOT the most stressful part of the workday."* Does that stop saved commute time from increasing output? No—the argument still stands.
  - Negate **(B)**: *"Remote workers DO lose more productive time to home distractions/delays than they save by not commuting."* If that's true, daily output will **drop**, completely destroying the conclusion! Thus **(B)** is the Necessary Assumption.

### 5. The 1 Common Mistake to Avoid
- **Trap**: Picking a strong, broad statement (`A`) instead of the **bridge statement** (`B`) whose negation makes the conclusion collapse.

## Concept 5: De-Nesting Double-Negative Stems (*"if false, would contradict... EXCEPT"*)

### 1. Concept & Rule in Plain English
CAT loves tying students in knots with double- or triple-negative question stems like:
- *"All of the following statements, **if false**, would **contradict** the passage **EXCEPT**:"*
- *"All of the following may be **rejected** as valid inferences **EXCEPT**:"*

**Never try to hold 3 negatives in your head while reading 30-word options!**
Take 5 seconds on your scratchpad to cancel pairs of negatives like minus signs in math (`(-) × (-) = (+)`):
1. *"If a statement $S$ being **false** **contradicts** the passage"* $\implies$ Statement $S$ itself **AGREES with (is TRUE in)** the passage!
2. Adding ***"EXCEPT"*** flips it once more: **"Find the 1 option that is ALREADY FALSE (contradicts the passage)!"**
3. Similarly, *"All may be **rejected** as valid inferences **EXCEPT**"* $=$ **"Find the 1 option that IS a valid inference!"**

### 2. When to Use It (Question / Text Cue)
- Any question stem containing two or more negative words (`false, contradict, reject, fail, undermine, incompatible, not, except`).

### 3. General Mini-Passage Example (Single Concept)
> *"In human experience, geographical places have a spiritual and emotional component in addition to their physical layout and topography."*
>
> **Question**: All of the following statements, **if false**, would **contradict** the sentence above, **EXCEPT**:
> - **(A)** Human experience of a geographical place extends beyond its physical layout.
> - **(B)** Places possess both physical topography and an emotional dimension for humans.
> - **(C)** Humans do not interact with places in emotional ways because places are only physical topography.

### 4. Step-by-Step Walkthrough (Two Ways)
- **Step 1 (Cancel Negatives on Scratchpad)**:
  - `if FALSE` ($-$) $\times$ `would CONTRADICT` ($-$) $=$ `TRUE in passage` ($+$).
  - With `EXCEPT` ($-$): **Find the option that is FALSE in the passage!**
- **Step 2 (Mark `T` or `F` next to each option)**:
  - **(A)**: `T` (Matches passage)
  - **(B)**: `T` (Matches passage)
  - **(C)**: `F` (Directly contradicts passage!) $\implies$ **Pick (C)**!

### 5. The 1 Common Mistake to Avoid
- **Trap**: Reading the options before translating the double-negative stem into a simple `Find TRUE` or `Find FALSE` instruction on your scratchpad!
